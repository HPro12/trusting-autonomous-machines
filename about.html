About this project

A public-interest project built around one hard question: when should any of us trust a self-driving car, and how would we know? This page covers who made it, why it exists, and how I researched it.

Why you should care

You don't have to own a self-driving car to live with the consequences of one. You share the road with them, you vote for the people who write their rules, and one day you may sit in the back seat with no one at the wheel. The technology is reaching public roads faster than most people's understanding of it, and that gap is where trust breaks.

There's a second reason, quieter and closer to home. Most cars sold today with "self-driving" features are Level 2: the car steers and brakes, but you are driving, every second. Plenty of people trust them like Level 4, the driverless kind private owners can't buy yet. That confusion is the line between a system that helps you drive and one you can hand your life to. Learning to tell the levels apart costs you nothing, and it may matter more than almost anything else you know about a car.

How it started

I watched a self-driving system struggle on a small road in the United States. Nothing dramatic happened. The car hesitated, second-guessed itself, and recovered. But I couldn't let go of the obvious question: is this safe enough? Doubt about one car pulled me down a much longer road, through crash data, sensors, regulation, ethics, and the strange distance between what these systems can do and what people believe they can do. It ended with me in China, interviewing the people who build and run these cars, because I wanted the whole picture instead of the marketing version of it.

The question

Ask an honest engineer whether a car is safe, and the honest answer is another question: safe where, safe when, and safe compared to what? Safety isn't a switch a vehicle flips on or off. So I stopped asking whether autonomous driving is safe and started asking something harder and more useful: when, and how much, should we trust it, and what would we need to see before we did?

The cars are already good enough to be on the road. The open question is whether the rest of us, riders, regulators, and neighbors, have the evidence and the understanding to trust them for the right reasons. That is the question I built this whole project to sit inside.

How I did it

I didn't want to stack one more opinion on top of the headlines. So the project rests on two things: what practitioners told me directly, and research careful enough to trace.

Primary interviews

Nine voices from inside the industry

Nine semi-structured interviews with autonomous-driving professionals across China, run in Chinese over 2025 and 2026: robotaxi operators, perception and planning engineers, data and deployment specialists, product managers, and a commercialization lead. I cite each of them by role, never by name, because candor was the price of usefulness and anonymity was the price of candor.

Desk research

A landscape you can trace

A reading of the regulatory and technical landscape as of 2026, organized around a knowledge graph I built across the interviews and their supporting material, so every claim traces back to a source.

What's real, what's staged

Invented drama, real numbers

The interactive chapters dramatize crashes and testimony so you feel the stakes. The numbers behind them, and the insights from the interviews, are real.

Who's behind this

My name is Haoyu Zhang (张皓禹), and I'm a student at Phillips Academy Andover. I grew up between Shanghai, Hong Kong, and the United States, which is probably why a question about an American road sent me looking for answers on Chinese ones. I'm not an industry insider, and this project doesn't pretend to be neutral about why it exists. I wanted to understand something that unsettled me, and the surest way I know to understand something is to build my way straight through it.

Credits & thanks

This project would not exist without the nine practitioners who gave me their time and their honesty. They stay anonymous here by design, described only by role, but nearly everything here rests on what they were willing to tell me. Thank you.
