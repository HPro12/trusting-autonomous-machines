Trust the Machine
Field interviews · anonymized

What the people building it actually said.

Nine practitioners working in China's autonomous-driving industry — engineers, operations, safety, and commercialization — on what earns public trust and what quietly erodes it.

Summaries are condensed from recorded interviews. Names and employers are withheld; each person is described only by role and organization type. Quotes are translated and tightened for length.

What they tend to agree on

The floor matters more than the ceiling

Trust rests on the worst case a system can produce, not its best. Regulators and engineers alike prize high redundancy and a safe fallback over a dazzling demo.

Trust is ridden, not argued

Almost everyone lands in the same place: people form fair judgments only after they personally ride one. Working in the industry doesn't grant trust automatically — experience does.

Run first, legislate later

Several describe China's approach as a whitelist that opens zone by zone once capability is proven — pragmatic, but leaving law, insurance, and liability lagging the technology.

The trolley problem is a distraction

To a vehicle the answer is usually just "brake." The harder, quieter problem is explainability — when a learned model errs, no one can fully reconstruct why, which makes liability murky.

Nine voices
01 · Operations

Robotaxi operations lead

Ride-hailing operator running robotaxis with a technology partner

  • Policy uncertainty is the biggest operational risk; one high-profile incident triggered nationwide tightening that disrupted rollout plans, cost, and pace.
  • Argues robotaxis are safer than regular taxis through enforced safety flows: the car won't move without a seatbelt, and stops itself if a door opens mid-trip.
  • Runs a mature tiered incident response (P0/P1/P2): in-car SOS, live remote operators, and ground crew dispatched to reach the scene within a set time.
  • Sees the L4-to-L5 gap as mostly algorithm, vision, and hardware maturity; today's service already covers roughly 80% of ride-hailing hotspots.
"Honestly I feel a robotaxi is actually safer than a regular taxi — its whole chain of safety-redundant procedures protects the passenger the entire trip."
02 · Engineering

Perception & decision engineer

Autonomous-driving technology company

  • Frames the L4 stack as a layered closed loop: sensor perception, intent prediction, finite-state decision, path planning, and vehicle-side control.
  • Handles corner cases by collecting rare scenarios and generating validated simulation data, then ranking threats by distance, speed, collision probability, and harm.
  • On no-win crashes there is no mature industry standard; the firm weights all lives equally and defaults to braking and avoidance rather than choosing whom to spare.
  • Names deep-learning explainability as the core bottleneck: unexplainable decisions make post-accident liability and reconstruction extremely hard.
"A rule you can derive mathematically; a deep-learning model is a huge pile of fitted parameters — meet data it has never seen and the output stops being explainable."
03 · Operations

Operations & regulatory strategy

Robotaxi operator

  • Reads China's regulation as a "whitelist": nothing is allowed until proven, then access opens zone by zone — trending steadily more open over time.
  • Most complaints aren't about safety but a user-education gap: fixed pickup points feel like bus stops beside a human driver's flexible curb stops.
  • Argues trust ultimately rests on solving the core need — moving safely from A to B at low cost, whatever the vehicle.
  • China's denser, more chaotic roads push operators toward rule-bound, safety-first strategies rather than the openness-first approach seen abroad.
"Regulators default to no, then grant you space once you prove a capability — so the trajectory is always toward more openness, never less."
04 · Engineering

Autonomy / planning engineer

AV software company

  • Treats driving as multi-objective constrained optimization: safety is a hard constraint, traffic rules a strong one, comfort and efficiency optimized within them.
  • Builds trust by making models strong enough to cover ~99% of scenes, then catching corner cases through data-return loops and simulation.
  • Believes the surest route to public confidence is more people personally riding, so the system is judged fairly rather than as "does everything" or "worse than a novice."
  • Flags an emerging risk: pedestrians exploiting guaranteed yielding, cutting across confident the car will always stop.
"Once people ride these cars themselves, they form a far more objective judgment of what the system can and cannot actually do."
05 · Data operations

Data operations & delivery lead

Robotaxi operator / AV data services

  • Calls the trolley problem a non-issue: for a vehicle the technical answer is simply to brake, not to perform moral calculus.
  • Separates consumer ADAS — a comfort feature, "70–80 points," never meant to solve A-to-B — from full robotaxi autonomy.
  • Sees trust as four dimensions — regulation, technology maturity, industrialization cost, and public cognition — and favors "run first, legislate later."
  • Warns the real hidden risk is that models keep improving while no one defines what a perfect "100 points" even looks like.
"Everyone advertises that the technology improved, but never tells you what 100 points should be — that undefined ceiling is the biggest hidden risk."
06 · Product

Product operations, robotaxi platform

Robotaxi operator

  • Speaks first as a driver: highway assist genuinely cuts fatigue, but city roads feel risky enough that he avoids engaging it.
  • Argues trust is earned incrementally through personal experience, not conferred automatically by working in the industry.
  • Stresses robotaxi value is geographically uneven and bound to its operating domain; forcing it into unsuitable areas creates traffic problems, not benefit.
  • Wants unified national legislation on top of traffic law, since fragmented local rules and divergent company definitions of edge cases threaten public safety.
"Without a clear higher-level standard, each company's race to define autonomy differently could create real threats to public traffic."
07 · Commercialization

AV commercialization lead

Robotaxi operator's L4 business unit

  • Public trust hinges on the safety floor, not the ceiling; regulators favor high-redundancy systems and confine vision-only "brute force" to simpler roads.
  • Sees a real split: a low-floor/high-ceiling data-driven path (very smart but occasionally dumb) versus a high-floor/lower-ceiling sensor-heavy path (safe but limited).
  • L4 deploys where the economics close, not where the tech allows — dense, standardized urban roads; rural and intercity routes fail on scale, jurisdiction, and rail competition.
  • Automakers own the full "golden" training data — auto-collected near-miss and collision events — and argues China needn't publish safety data publicly, unlike the US disclosure model.
"Facing anything new we're contradictory: it must never cause an accident, yet we also want it perfectly idealized — that tension is why both approaches survive at once."
08 · Engineering

AV algorithm engineer

Robotaxi operator running L4 vehicles from third-party AV firms

  • Safety is the first-order metric across L2–L4; decision modules are moving from modular pipelines toward end-to-end models trained to imitate human driving.
  • Handles known corner cases — darting pedestrians, extreme weather — through targeted data collection, simulation, and data synthesis.
  • Rejects engineering the trolley problem; models converge to the aggregate of human choices via shadow-mode comparison, treating human decisions as ground truth.
  • Believes the public over-fears machine error while tolerating human error, sensing a "loss of control" anxiety that wider, calmer adoption could ease.
"People accept that humans err as fate or bad luck, but they can't accept that a machine makes mistakes too."
09 · Product

Product & technology manager

Robotaxi operator

  • Says the main barrier to L5 isn't technology, which is nearing maturity, but law, insurance, liability rules, and public acceptance.
  • Distinguishes safety (quantifiable, rule-controllable) from risk (probabilistic, never zero); trolley-type dilemmas are rare — an ethics and regulation issue more than an algorithm one.
  • Ranks safety first, then rules; the system can "downgrade" — break a rule to avoid a child — rather than choose between two fixed options.
  • Notes speed and zone limits are phased and set by road law, matching human limits, so they don't meaningfully hurt commercialization.
"The technology is basically there; what still lags is law, insurance, liability, and the public's willingness to just get in without a second thought."
