# How Should China Build Public Trust in Autonomous Driving?

**A policy white paper — Version 1 (formal, for Chinese-industry readers)**
Part of the *Trust the Machine* research initiative

---

## About this outline

This is the structural outline for the formal policy report. It is one of three components of the
*Trust the Machine* initiative — alongside the primary-research interviews with nine professionals
in China's autonomous-driving (AD) ecosystem and the five-chapter interactive website.

**The report's job:** answer one question — *how should China build **justified** public trust in
autonomous driving?* — as solution-oriented policy analysis, not a description of regulations.

**Guiding question for every section:** *how does this help society build justified trust in
autonomous driving?* If a passage does not contribute to that, it is cut.

**Central thesis:** technology creates capability; policy creates accountability; education creates
trust. AD scales responsibly only when all three grow together.

**Production decisions:** English · delivered as DOCX · interviewees anonymized by role · sober
"Deliberate" palette matching the website.

## Report structure

### 0. Executive Summary
Thesis; the reframed question; the trust equation; a one-paragraph TRUST preview; the five headline
recommendations, one per pillar. *(Written last.)*

### 1. Introduction — Reframing the Question
From *"Is AD safe?"* to *"What institutions, evidence, and communication let society determine
whether AD is **safe enough**?"* The trust equation (capability → accountability → trust). Scope
(China, as of 2026), method note (nine anonymized interviews + desk research + knowledge graph),
and the report's relationship to the website.

### 2. The Current Landscape
- **Governance today:** deploy-first (先跑先试), the **whitelist** model, **ring-based** rollout,
  government-gated ODDs, incident grading (P0/P1/P2), the **Wuhan flashpoint** and the nationwide
  tightening it triggered.
- **Technology status:** "commercially usable"; L2-comfort-feature vs. L4-autonomy gap; two
  technical routes (rule/HD-map/cloud-heavy vs. end-to-end).
- **Where trust comes from today:** China's managed / state-trust model (limited mandated
  disclosure; the NEV transition used as precedent).

### 3. Key Challenges — The Trust Deficit
Five interlocking problems, each mapping to a TRUST pillar:
- **3.1 The Evidence Problem** *(→ Safety Validation)* — the long tail; the statistical
  near-impossibility of *proving* safety; corner cases; "solves 98 of 100, fails 2."
- **3.2 The Transparency Problem** *(→ Transparency)* — no mandated disclosure; model
  interpretability / the black box (the top liability bottleneck named in interviews); the
  undefined ceiling ("nobody tells you what 100 looks like").
- **3.3 The Communication Problem** *(→ Understanding)* — L2 marketing vs. reality; non-standard
  terminology; firms defining edge cases differently; asymmetric error tolerance.
- **3.4 The Accountability Problem** *(→ Responsibility)* — L2/L3/L4 liability; handover;
  reconstructing a black-box decision for liability.
- **3.5 The Human-Factors Problem** *(cross-cutting)* — automation complacency; the exposure gap;
  pickup/dropoff friction; public fears (jobs, loss of intervention); AV-induced behavior.

### 4. Comparative Analysis *(case studies, not rankings)*
United States (public disclosure; NGO/insurer/media intermediaries; default-allow) · Germany (L3
law and type approval) · Singapore (structured sandbox, national standards) · Japan (cautious,
standards-led). Then: **what China could realistically adopt** given its legal system, industrial
policy, and managed-trust model — honest about what does *not* transfer.

### 5. The TRUST Framework *(signature contribution)*
The five pillars and **why they reinforce one another**:
- **T — Transparency** · standardized reporting of capabilities, ODDs, limitations, disengagement.
- **R — Responsibility** · legal responsibility across L2/L3/L4, kept understandable to consumers.
- **U — Understanding** · public technological literacy; educational tools including the website.
- **S — Safety Validation** · standardized testing, benchmarks, third-party audits, monitoring.
- **T — Trustworthy Governance** · adaptive regulation balancing innovation, accountability, confidence.

### 6. Recommendations
Five top-tier recommendations, one per pillar, each absorbing the measures that serve it and each
with the same five fields — **Why it matters · Who implements · Expected benefits · Tradeoffs ·
Implementation challenges**:
- **R1 — Transparency:** standardized disclosure, anchored by the two-tier **Autonomy Label** (hero) — plus machine-readable ODD disclosure and standardized disengagement/incident reporting.
- **R2 — Responsibility:** clear ex-ante liability by level, made enforceable by event-data recording and reconstruction standards.
- **R3 — Understanding:** a national public-understanding program (including the website) built on unified, standardized terminology.
- **R4 — Safety Validation:** independent validation before and after deployment — certification, third-party auditing and data platforms, and post-deployment monitoring.
- **R5 — Trustworthy Governance:** adaptive regulation on a stable safety floor, learned through formal regulatory sandboxes. (Explainability applied where proportionate — the near-term lever is R2's reconstruction standards.)

### 7. Future Outlook
L4→L5 is not linear; inter-city and cross-jurisdiction barriers; the floor-vs-ceiling tension
(Waymo "strong eyes, child's brain" vs. Tesla "few senses, expert brain") and its governance
implication; how the managed-trust model might evolve toward evidence-based trust.

### 8. Conclusion
Technology → capability; policy → accountability; education → trust. Restate the reframed question
and the flywheel.
